Question: How can we apply the ethics of Homomorphic Encryption using Cultural Relativism?

Short answer: You can apply cultural relativism to the ethics of homomorphic encryption (HE) by evaluating the technology’s ethical implications within the cultural context in which it is deployed, rather than assuming a universal ethical standard. This means recognizing that different societies will interpret privacy, accountability, transparency, and acceptable trade offs in distinct ways — and those cultural differences should shape how HE is governed and evaluated.
How Cultural Relativism Reframes the Ethics of Homomorphic Encryption
Cultural relativism says that ethical judgments must be understood within the norms, values, and worldview of a specific culture. Homomorphic encryption raises ethical issues around privacy, accountability, transparency, and trust — all of which are culturally shaped.
1. Privacy Expectations Are Culturally Defined
HE is often praised for strong privacy protection, especially in healthcare and AI systems. But privacy norms vary widely across cultures:
Some societies treat personal data as highly sensitive and expect strict confidentiality.
Others prioritize communal benefit (e.g., public health research) over individual privacy.
HE’s privacy preserving benefits may be seen as ethically “good” in cultures that emphasize individual autonomy, but in collectivist cultures, the same strict privacy could be viewed as obstructing beneficial data sharing. 

2. Accountability and Transparency Expectations Differ Across Cultures
A major ethical concern with HE is that encrypted computation can make AI systems more opaque, complicating accountability and trust. Cultural relativism helps explain why this matters differently in different societies:
Cultures with strong expectations of institutional transparency may view HE based opaque models as ethically problematic.
Cultures with high trust in centralized authorities may be more comfortable with reduced visibility into algorithmic decision making.

3. Different Cultures Prioritize Ethical Trade offs Differently
HE introduces trade offs: stronger privacy but weaker explainability; secure computation but higher computational cost. Cultural relativism suggests that:
Some cultures may prioritize privacy above all else.
Others may prioritize efficiency, public benefit, or institutional oversight.
Thus, the “ethical balance” of HE is not universal — it depends on cultural values.

4. Cultural Context Shapes Perceptions of Risk
In healthcare, HE is used to protect patient data while enabling research. Ethical evaluations of this depend on cultural attitudes toward medical institutions, data sharing, and trust. For example, expert assessments show that HE in health data sharing raises concerns about governance, fairness, and legal norms — all of which differ across societies. 

5. Cultural Relativism Helps Interpret Ethical Concerns About Genomic Data
Genomic data sharing using HE and blockchain raises culturally sensitive issues: identity, ancestry, and community rights. Ethical perspectives differ across cultures regarding:
Who “owns” genomic data
Whether individuals or communities should control access
How privacy should be balanced with scientific progress These issues are highlighted in ethical analyses of HE based genomic data systems. 

Putting It Together: A Framework
Here’s how cultural relativism can be applied systematically:

Step 1 — Identify cultural values related to privacy, trust, and technology.
Different cultures define “ethical” differently.

Step 2 — Evaluate how HE aligns or conflicts with those values.
Does HE support culturally important privacy norms? Does it undermine expectations of transparency?

Step 3 — Assess culturally specific risks and benefits.
For example, in cultures with low trust in institutions, HE’s opacity may be a major ethical concern.

Step 4 — Adapt governance and policy to cultural context.
Ethical guidelines for HE should not be one size fits all.

Final Takeaway

Cultural relativism doesn’t change the technical properties of homomorphic encryption — it changes how we judge its ethical acceptability. It reminds us that privacy, accountability, and trust are culturally shaped, and therefore the ethics of HE must be evaluated within the cultural context where the technology is deployed.
